Lesson 2: Carboxylic Acids, Esters, Amines, and Amides

Overview

In this lesson, we explore four major functional groups essential in biology and organic chemistry:

  • Carboxylic Acids (−COOH)
  • Esters (−COOR)
  • Amines (−NH₂)
  • Amides (−CONH₂)
These groups are especially important in biomolecules such as:
  • Fatty acids → Carboxylic acids
  • Proteins → Amides (peptide bonds)
  • DNA bases → Amines

1. Carboxylic Acids (−COOH)

Carboxylic acids contain both a carbonyl (C=O) and a hydroxyl (−OH) group attached to the same carbon.

General Structure: R−COOH

Why are they acidic?

They can donate a hydrogen ion (H⁺) because the resulting carboxylate ion is stabilized by resonance.

Quick Check: Why is the carboxylate ion stable after losing H⁺?
Because the negative charge is delocalized over two oxygen atoms (resonance stabilization).

Examples

  • Acetic acid (vinegar)
  • Fatty acids

2. Esters (−COOR)

Esters are formed from the reaction of a carboxylic acid and an alcohol.

Formation (Esterification)

Carboxylic Acid + Alcohol → Ester + Water

Quick Check: What type of reaction forms esters?
A condensation reaction (removal of water).

Properties

  • Often have pleasant smells (fruity)
  • Less polar than acids

3. Amines (−NH₂)

Amines are nitrogen-containing compounds derived from ammonia (NH₃).

Types

  • Primary (R−NH₂)
  • Secondary (R₂NH)
  • Tertiary (R₃N)

Amines are basic because nitrogen has a lone pair of electrons.

Quick Check: Why do amines act as bases?
Because the nitrogen atom has a lone pair that can accept a proton (H⁺).

4. Amides (−CONH₂)

Amides are formed from carboxylic acids and amines.

Importance

Amides form peptide bonds, which connect amino acids in proteins.

Quick Check: What bond links amino acids in proteins?
Peptide bond (an amide bond).
