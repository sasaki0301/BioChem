Lesson 3: Disaccharides and Polysaccharides

Monosaccharides can join together to form larger carbohydrates. In this lesson, you will learn how disaccharides and polysaccharides are formed, broken down, and used in living organisms.

Overview — What You Will Learn

  • What disaccharides and polysaccharides are
  • How monosaccharides join through glycosidic bonds
  • The difference between condensation and hydrolysis
  • Common disaccharides: maltose, sucrose, and lactose
  • Common polysaccharides: starch, glycogen, cellulose, and chitin
  • How bond type affects carbohydrate structure and function
Big Idea: The same building block, glucose, can form very different molecules depending on how it is connected. Starch, glycogen, and cellulose are all made of glucose, but they behave differently because of their bond patterns and shapes.

1. From Monosaccharides to Larger Carbohydrates

A monosaccharide is a single sugar unit, such as glucose, fructose, or galactose. When monosaccharides join together, they form larger carbohydrates.

  • Disaccharide: two monosaccharides joined together
  • Oligosaccharide: short chain of several monosaccharides
  • Polysaccharide: long chain of many monosaccharides
Two monosaccharides can join to form a disaccharide.
Quick Check: What is the main difference between a monosaccharide and a disaccharide?
A monosaccharide has one sugar unit, while a disaccharide has two monosaccharides joined together.

2. Glycosidic Bonds: The Link Between Sugars

The bond that connects sugar units is called a glycosidic bond. It usually forms between the anomeric carbon of one sugar and a hydroxyl group of another sugar.

Glycosidic bonds are important because they determine the structure, digestibility, and function of carbohydrates.

Key idea: A glycosidic bond is to carbohydrates what a peptide bond is to proteins. It is the linkage that connects the building blocks.
A glycosidic bond forms through condensation, releasing water.
Quick Check: What molecule is released when a glycosidic bond forms?
Water is released during glycosidic bond formation.

3. Condensation and Hydrolysis

Carbohydrates are built and broken down by two opposite types of reactions:

Reaction What Happens? Carbohydrate Example
Condensation Two sugars join together and water is removed. Glucose + fructose → sucrose + water
Hydrolysis Water is added to break a glycosidic bond. Sucrose + water → glucose + fructose
Condensation builds carbohydrates; hydrolysis breaks carbohydrates.
Quick Check: Which reaction is used during digestion to break carbohydrates into smaller sugars?
Hydrolysis breaks glycosidic bonds during digestion.

4. Common Disaccharides

Disaccharides are made of two monosaccharides joined by a glycosidic bond. Even when two disaccharides have the same number of sugar units, they can behave differently depending on the exact monosaccharides and bond type.

Disaccharide Monosaccharide Components Common Source Important Note
Maltose Glucose + Glucose Germinating grains; starch breakdown Product of partial starch digestion
Sucrose Glucose + Fructose Table sugar; sugar cane; sugar beets Non-reducing sugar
Lactose Glucose + Galactose Milk and dairy products Requires lactase enzyme for digestion
Common confusion: Lactose and sucrose are both disaccharides, but lactose is glucose + galactose, while sucrose is glucose + fructose.
Quick Check: Which disaccharide is found in milk?
Lactose is the sugar found in milk.

5. Reducing and Non-Reducing Disaccharides

A disaccharide may be reducing or non-reducing depending on whether it has a free anomeric carbon.

  • Maltose: reducing
  • Lactose: reducing
  • Sucrose: non-reducing

Sucrose is non-reducing because both anomeric carbons are involved in the glycosidic bond, so neither sugar unit can open into a reactive carbonyl form.

Quick Check: Why is sucrose non-reducing?
Because both anomeric carbons are locked in the glycosidic bond.

6. What Are Polysaccharides?

Polysaccharides are long chains of monosaccharides joined by glycosidic bonds. They may be straight or branched.

Polysaccharides usually serve two major roles:

  • Storage: storing glucose for later energy use
  • Structure: providing strength and support
A polysaccharide is a long chain of many monosaccharides.

7. Storage Polysaccharides: Starch and Glycogen

Starch

Starch is the main storage polysaccharide in plants. It is made of glucose units connected mainly by α-glycosidic bonds.

Starch has two major components:

  • Amylose: mostly linear chain
  • Amylopectin: branched chain

Glycogen

Glycogen is the main storage polysaccharide in animals. It is stored mainly in the liver and muscles.

Glycogen is highly branched, allowing glucose to be released quickly when the body needs energy.

Feature Starch Glycogen
Main organism Plants Animals
Function Energy storage Energy storage
Structure Linear and branched forms Highly branched
Advantage Stores glucose in plant tissues Rapid glucose release when needed
Quick Check: Why is branching useful in glycogen?
Branching creates many ends where enzymes can quickly remove glucose units, allowing fast energy release.

8. Structural Polysaccharides: Cellulose and Chitin

Cellulose

Cellulose is a structural polysaccharide found in plant cell walls. It is made of glucose units connected by β-glycosidic bonds.

Humans cannot digest cellulose because we do not have the enzyme needed to break its β-linkages. However, cellulose is still important in the human diet as dietary fiber.

Chitin

Chitin is a structural polysaccharide found in fungal cell walls and in the exoskeletons of arthropods such as insects and crustaceans.

Important comparison: Starch and cellulose are both made of glucose, but starch has α-linkages while cellulose has β-linkages. This changes their shape, digestibility, and function.
Polysaccharide Main Function Where Found Digestibility by Humans
Starch Energy storage Plants Digestible
Glycogen Energy storage Animals Digestible
Cellulose Structural support Plant cell walls Not digestible by humans
Chitin Structural support Fungi and arthropods Not normally digestible by humans
Quick Check: Why can humans digest starch but not cellulose?
Humans have enzymes that break α-glycosidic bonds in starch, but not the β-glycosidic bonds in cellulose.

10. Practice Activities

Activity 1: Identify the Disaccharide

Identify the disaccharide formed from each pair:

  1. Glucose + glucose
  2. Glucose + fructose
  3. Glucose + galactose
  1. Maltose
  2. Sucrose
  3. Lactose

Activity 2: Storage or Structure?

Classify each as storage or structural polysaccharide:

  1. Starch
  2. Glycogen
  3. Cellulose
  4. Chitin
  1. Starch — storage
  2. Glycogen — storage
  3. Cellulose — structural
  4. Chitin — structural

Activity 3: Explain the Difference

Starch and cellulose are both made of glucose. Why do they have different functions?

They have different glycosidic linkages. Starch has α-linkages that form digestible energy-storage chains, while cellulose has β-linkages that form strong structural fibers.

Activity 4: Digestion Application

A person lacks lactase enzyme. Which disaccharide will be difficult to digest, and what are its components?

Lactose will be difficult to digest. It is made of glucose and galactose.

Activity 5: Reaction Identification

What reaction breaks maltose into two glucose molecules?

Hydrolysis breaks maltose into two glucose molecules.

11. Key Terms Summary

  • Disaccharide: Carbohydrate made of two monosaccharides.
  • Polysaccharide: Long carbohydrate chain made of many monosaccharides.
  • Glycosidic Bond: Bond linking sugar units together.
  • Condensation: Reaction that joins molecules and releases water.
  • Hydrolysis: Reaction that breaks bonds using water.
  • Maltose: Glucose + glucose.
  • Sucrose: Glucose + fructose.
  • Lactose: Glucose + galactose.
  • Starch: Plant storage polysaccharide.
  • Glycogen: Animal storage polysaccharide.
  • Cellulose: Plant structural polysaccharide.
  • Chitin: Structural polysaccharide in fungi and arthropods.
