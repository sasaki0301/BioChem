Big Idea: Enzymes are biological catalysts that make life possible by speeding up chemical reactions without being used up.

1. What Are Enzymes?

Enzymes are biological molecules that speed up chemical reactions inside living organisms. Most enzymes are proteins, although some RNA molecules can also act as catalysts.

A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being consumed. This means enzymes can be used again and again after each reaction.

Example: Amylase is an enzyme in saliva that helps break down starch into simpler sugars.
Quick Check: Why are enzymes considered catalysts?
Because they speed up chemical reactions without being used up or permanently changed.

2. Why Living Organisms Need Enzymes

Cells carry out thousands of chemical reactions every second. These reactions include digestion, energy production, DNA copying, waste removal, and the building of new molecules.

Many reactions would happen too slowly at normal body temperature. The body cannot simply raise its temperature to speed reactions because excessive heat can damage cells and denature proteins.

Simple analogy: If a reaction is like pushing a heavy rock over a hill, an enzyme makes the hill lower so the reaction can happen more easily.
Quick Check: Why can’t the body just use high temperature to speed up reactions?
High temperature can damage cells and denature proteins, including enzymes.

3. Activation Energy

Before a chemical reaction can occur, molecules must overcome an energy barrier called activation energy.

Activation energy is the minimum energy needed to start a reaction. Enzymes lower this energy requirement, allowing reactions to happen faster under normal biological conditions.

Enzymes lower activation energy, allowing reactions to occur more easily.

Quick Check: Do enzymes add energy to reactions?
No. Enzymes lower the activation energy needed for the reaction to proceed.

4. Enzyme Specificity

Enzymes are highly specific. This means each enzyme usually works on one substrate or a small group of closely related substrates.

A substrate is the molecule that an enzyme acts on.

EnzymeSubstrateFunction
AmylaseStarchBreaks starch into simpler sugars
LactaseLactoseBreaks lactose into glucose and galactose
LipaseLipidsBreaks fats into fatty acids and glycerol
ProteaseProteinsBreaks proteins into peptides or amino acids
Real-life connection: People with lactose intolerance do not produce enough lactase, so lactose is not digested properly.
Quick Check: Why can’t amylase digest proteins?
Because amylase is specific for starch and does not recognize proteins as its substrate.

5. Enzyme-Substrate Complex

When an enzyme binds to its substrate, they form a temporary structure called the enzyme-substrate complex.

After the reaction occurs, the product is released and the enzyme is free to act again.

General reaction:
Enzyme + Substrate → Enzyme-Substrate Complex → Enzyme + Product

The enzyme binds the substrate, forms a complex, releases the product, and remains reusable.

Quick Check: What happens to the enzyme after the product is released?
The enzyme remains unchanged and can be used again in another reaction.

6. Importance of Enzymes in Living Organisms

Enzymes are involved in almost every biological process. Without enzymes, reactions needed for life would be too slow.

Biological ProcessExample EnzymeRole
DigestionAmylase, lipase, proteaseBreak down food molecules
Energy productionRespiratory enzymesHelp produce ATP
DNA replicationDNA polymeraseCopies DNA
ProtectionCatalaseBreaks down harmful hydrogen peroxide
Quick Check: Why would life be difficult without enzymes?
Many essential reactions would occur too slowly to support life.

7. Real-World Applications of Enzymes

  • Medicine: enzymes are used in blood tests, diagnostics, and drug development.
  • Food industry: enzymes help in cheese production, bread making, and fermentation.
  • Biotechnology: DNA polymerase is used in PCR to copy DNA.
  • Household products: detergent enzymes break down protein, starch, and fat stains.
Application: Enzymes are useful because they are specific, efficient, and work under mild conditions.

9. Practice Activities

Activity 1: Correct the Statement

A student says, “Enzymes are used up during reactions.” Is this correct? Explain.

The statement is incorrect. Enzymes are not consumed during reactions. They help convert substrates into products and can be reused afterward.

Activity 2: Lactose Intolerance Scenario

A person experiences gas and bloating after drinking milk. Answer the following:

  1. Which enzyme may be lacking?
  2. What substrate is not being digested properly?
  3. Why do symptoms occur?
  1. The enzyme is lactase.
  2. The substrate is lactose.
  3. Symptoms occur because undigested lactose is fermented by intestinal bacteria, producing gas and discomfort.

Activity 3: ATP Production Problem

If an enzyme needed for ATP production stops working, predict two possible effects on the cell.

The cell may produce less ATP, and energy-dependent processes such as active transport, repair, growth, and movement may slow down or stop.

Activity 4: Challenge Reasoning

A scientist discovers a biological molecule that speeds up a reaction and remains unchanged afterward. Why might it be classified as an enzyme?

It behaves like a catalyst because it increases reaction rate without being consumed. If it works in a biological system, it may be classified as an enzyme.

10. Key Terms

  • Enzyme: Biological catalyst that speeds up reactions.
  • Catalyst: Substance that increases reaction rate without being consumed.
  • Activation Energy: Minimum energy needed to start a reaction.
  • Substrate: Molecule acted upon by an enzyme.
  • Product: Molecule formed after the reaction.
  • Enzyme-Substrate Complex: Temporary binding of enzyme and substrate.
  • Specificity: Ability of an enzyme to act on a particular substrate.
