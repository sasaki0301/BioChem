Classification and Types of Enzymes

Big Idea: Thousands of enzymes exist in living organisms. Scientists classify them according to the type of reaction they catalyze.

1. Why Are Enzymes Classified?

Scientists have identified thousands of enzymes in living organisms. Because there are so many enzymes, a systematic way of organizing them is needed.

Rather than classifying enzymes by where they are found, scientists classify them according to the chemical reactions they catalyze.

For example, digestive enzymes and cellular enzymes may be found in different parts of the body, but they can belong to the same enzyme class if they perform the same type of reaction.

Think About It: Books in a library are organized by subject rather than color. Similarly, enzymes are classified by function rather than location.
Quick Check: Why do scientists classify enzymes based on reactions rather than location?
Classification by reaction type provides a consistent way to organize enzymes because the same type of reaction may occur in different organisms and tissues.

2. Enzyme Naming Convention

Most enzyme names end with the suffix -ase.

Many enzymes are named after the substrate they act upon.

Substrate Enzyme Function
Starch Amylase Breaks down starch
Lactose Lactase Breaks down lactose
Lipids Lipase Breaks down fats
Proteins Protease Breaks down proteins

However, some enzymes have historical names that do not follow this rule.

Examples include:

  • Pepsin
  • Trypsin
  • Chymotrypsin
Quick Check: If an enzyme breaks down cellulose, what would its likely name be?
Cellulase, because enzyme names commonly end in "-ase" and are often named after their substrate.

3. The Six Major Classes of Enzymes

The International Union of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology (IUBMB) classifies enzymes into six major groups based on the reactions they catalyze.

Scientists classify enzymes according to the reactions they catalyze.

Class Function
Oxidoreductases Transfer electrons
Transferases Transfer functional groups
Hydrolases Break bonds using water
Lyases Add or remove groups without hydrolysis
Isomerases Rearrange atoms within molecules
Ligases Join molecules together using ATP
Quick Check: Which enzyme class would most digestive enzymes belong to?
Most digestive enzymes belong to the hydrolase class because they break large molecules into smaller ones using water.

4. Oxidoreductases

Oxidoreductases catalyze oxidation-reduction (redox) reactions involving electron transfer.

These reactions are extremely important in cellular respiration and energy production.

Example: Dehydrogenase enzymes remove hydrogen atoms and electrons from molecules during cellular respiration.

Without oxidoreductases, cells would not be able to efficiently produce ATP.

Quick Check: Why are oxidoreductases important for energy production?
They facilitate electron transfer reactions that ultimately drive ATP production.

5. Transferases

Transferases transfer functional groups from one molecule to another.

These groups may include:

  • Phosphate groups
  • Methyl groups
  • Amino groups
Example: Hexokinase transfers a phosphate group from ATP to glucose during glycolysis.

This reaction is one of the first steps in cellular energy production.

Quick Check: Which enzyme class would add a phosphate group to a molecule?
Transferases, specifically kinases, transfer phosphate groups.

6. Hydrolases

Hydrolases catalyze reactions that break chemical bonds by adding water.

This process is called hydrolysis.

Enzyme Substrate Product
Amylase Starch Simple sugars
Lipase Lipids Fatty acids and glycerol
Protease Proteins Amino acids
Important: Most digestive enzymes are hydrolases.
Quick Check: Why is water necessary for hydrolysis reactions?
Water participates directly in breaking chemical bonds during hydrolysis.

7. Lyases, Isomerases, and Ligases

Lyases

Lyases add or remove groups from molecules without using water or oxidation.

They often form or break double bonds.

Isomerases

Isomerases rearrange atoms within a molecule to form an isomer.

Example: During glycolysis, phosphoglucose isomerase converts glucose-6-phosphate into fructose-6-phosphate.

Ligases

Ligases join two molecules together using energy from ATP.

Example: DNA ligase joins DNA fragments during DNA replication and repair.
Quick Check: Which enzyme class would help repair a broken DNA strand?
Ligases, particularly DNA ligase, join DNA fragments together.

8. The Newer Seventh Class: Translocases

In 2018, the IUBMB introduced a seventh enzyme class called translocases.

Translocases move ions or molecules across biological membranes.

Example: ATP synthase helps transport protons across membranes while producing ATP.

Although newer than the traditional six classes, translocases are essential for cellular transport and energy production.

Quick Check: What is the primary function of translocases?
They transport molecules or ions across biological membranes.

9. Why Enzyme Classification Matters

Understanding enzyme classification helps scientists:

  • Predict enzyme function
  • Study metabolic pathways
  • Develop medicines
  • Diagnose diseases
  • Design industrial applications

For example, many drugs work by inhibiting specific enzyme classes involved in disease processes.

Real-World Application: Many cholesterol-lowering drugs target enzymes involved in cholesterol synthesis.

11. Practice Activities

Activity 1: Classification Challenge

A scientist discovers an enzyme that breaks proteins into amino acids by adding water. Which enzyme class does it belong to? Explain.

The enzyme belongs to the hydrolase class because it uses water to break chemical bonds.

Activity 2: Real-Life Application

A patient has difficulty digesting fats. Which enzyme is most likely deficient and what enzyme class does it belong to?

The enzyme is likely lipase. Lipase belongs to the hydrolase class because it breaks down fats through hydrolysis.

Activity 3: Metabolism Connection

Hexokinase transfers a phosphate group from ATP to glucose. Which enzyme class does it belong to and why?

Hexokinase is a transferase because it transfers a phosphate group from one molecule to another.

Activity 4: Higher-Order Challenge

DNA ligase becomes nonfunctional in a cell. Predict two possible consequences during DNA replication.

DNA fragments may not join properly, leading to incomplete DNA replication and increased risk of mutations or cell death.
