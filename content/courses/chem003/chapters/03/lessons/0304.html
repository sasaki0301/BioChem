Lesson 4: Chemical Properties of Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are not only energy molecules. Their chemical properties allow them to react, form bonds, break down, change shape, and participate in biological processes.

Overview — What You Will Learn

  • Why carbohydrates are reactive because of hydroxyl and carbonyl groups
  • The meaning of reducing and non-reducing sugars
  • Oxidation and reduction of carbohydrates
  • Hydrolysis of glycosidic bonds
  • Condensation reactions that build larger carbohydrates
  • Isomerization and mutarotation
  • Biological importance of carbohydrate reactions
Big Idea: Most carbohydrate reactions come from two major features: many −OH groups and a reactive carbonyl/anomeric carbon.

1. Why Are Carbohydrates Chemically Reactive?

Carbohydrates contain several hydroxyl groups and usually one carbonyl group in their open-chain form. These groups allow carbohydrates to form hydrogen bonds, react with oxidizing agents, form glycosidic bonds, and change between open-chain and ring forms.

Structural Feature Chemical Effect Example
Many −OH groups Increase polarity and allow hydrogen bonding. Glucose dissolves well in water.
Carbonyl group Allows oxidation/reduction and ring formation. Open-chain glucose can act as a reducing sugar.
Anomeric carbon Determines reducing ability and glycosidic bond formation. Sucrose is non-reducing because both anomeric carbons are bonded.
Quick Check: Which two functional features make carbohydrates chemically active?
Hydroxyl groups and a carbonyl/anomeric carbon.

2. Reducing Sugars

A reducing sugar is a carbohydrate that can reduce another substance while it itself becomes oxidized. In simple terms, reducing sugars can donate electrons to mild oxidizing agents.

A sugar is usually reducing if it has a free anomeric carbon. This means the ring can open into an open-chain form with a reactive carbonyl group.

Reducing sugars can open into a reactive carbonyl form.

Examples of Reducing Sugars

  • Glucose
  • Fructose
  • Galactose
  • Maltose
  • Lactose

Example of a Non-Reducing Sugar

  • Sucrose
Why is sucrose non-reducing?
Sucrose has both anomeric carbons involved in its glycosidic bond, so it cannot freely open into a reactive carbonyl form.
Quick Check: Why is maltose reducing but sucrose is not?
Maltose has one free anomeric carbon, while sucrose has both anomeric carbons locked in the glycosidic bond.

3. Oxidation of Carbohydrates

Oxidation occurs when a carbohydrate loses electrons, gains oxygen, or loses hydrogen. Reducing sugars can be oxidized because their open-chain forms have reactive carbonyl groups.

In many textbook discussions, glucose can be oxidized to form gluconic acid when its aldehyde group is converted into a carboxylic acid group.

The aldehyde group of glucose can be oxidized to a carboxylic acid group.
Conceptual test connection: Benedict’s and Fehling’s concepts are based on reducing sugars donating electrons to copper ions, causing a visible color change in laboratory discussions.
Quick Check: Which part of glucose is oxidized in reducing sugar reactions?
The reactive carbonyl group in the open-chain form is oxidized.

4. Reduction of Carbohydrates

Reduction is the opposite of oxidation. In carbohydrate chemistry, reduction can convert a carbonyl group into an alcohol group.

For example, glucose can be reduced to sorbitol, a sugar alcohol. This is important in food chemistry and also has biological relevance.

Starting Sugar Reduced Product Type of Product
Glucose Sorbitol Sugar alcohol
Galactose Galactitol Sugar alcohol
Ribose Ribitol Sugar alcohol
Quick Check: What functional group is commonly converted into an alcohol during carbohydrate reduction?
The carbonyl group is reduced into an alcohol group.

5. Hydrolysis of Carbohydrates

Hydrolysis is the process of breaking bonds using water. In carbohydrates, hydrolysis breaks glycosidic bonds.

This is especially important in digestion because large carbohydrates must be broken into smaller sugars before they can be absorbed.

Hydrolysis uses water to break glycosidic bonds.

Examples

  • Sucrose + water → glucose + fructose
  • Lactose + water → glucose + galactose
  • Starch + water → shorter glucose chains, then glucose
Quick Check: What bond is broken during carbohydrate hydrolysis?
The glycosidic bond is broken.

6. Condensation Reactions

Condensation is the opposite of hydrolysis. It joins sugar units together and releases water.

This is how disaccharides and polysaccharides are formed from monosaccharides.

Condensation forms glycosidic bonds and releases water.
Quick Check: Which reaction builds disaccharides from monosaccharides?
Condensation builds disaccharides from monosaccharides.

7. Isomerization of Sugars

Some sugars have the same molecular formula but different structures. These are called isomers.

For example, glucose, fructose, and galactose all have the formula C6H12O6, but their atoms are arranged differently.

Sugar Formula Difference
Glucose C6H12O6 Aldohexose
Fructose C6H12O6 Ketohexose
Galactose C6H12O6 Different spatial arrangement from glucose
Important: Same formula does not mean same function. Different shapes affect sweetness, digestion, enzyme recognition, and biological role.
Quick Check: Why are glucose and fructose considered isomers?
They have the same molecular formula but different structures.

8. Mutarotation

Mutarotation is the change in optical rotation that occurs when alpha and beta forms of a sugar interconvert in solution.

This happens because the cyclic form can briefly open to the open-chain form, then close again as either the alpha or beta anomer.

Mutarotation occurs as alpha and beta anomers interconvert through the open-chain form.
Quick Check: What two forms interconvert during mutarotation?
Alpha and beta anomers interconvert.

9. Biological Importance of Carbohydrate Reactions

Carbohydrate reactions are important in living systems because they support energy production, digestion, storage, and cell communication.

Reaction or Property Biological Importance
Hydrolysis Digests starch, glycogen, lactose, and sucrose into absorbable sugars.
Condensation Builds disaccharides, starch, glycogen, and cellulose.
Reducing ability Helps classify sugars and is related to open-chain carbonyl forms.
Isomerization Allows conversion between sugars in metabolic pathways.
Mutarotation Explains why sugars can shift between alpha and beta forms in solution.

Sugar−O−Sugar + H₂O → Sugar−OH + HO−Sugar

Hydrolysis, because water is used to break a glycosidic bond.

Activity 3: Explain Briefly

Why is sucrose considered non-reducing?

Sucrose is non-reducing because both anomeric carbons are involved in the glycosidic bond, so no free anomeric carbon is available.

Activity 4: Isomer Classification

Glucose and fructose both have the formula C6H12O6. Why are they different molecules?

They have the same molecular formula but different structures. Glucose is an aldose, while fructose is a ketose.

Activity 5: Application

A sugar can open into a form with a free carbonyl group. What property will it likely have?

It will likely be a reducing sugar.

12. Key Terms Summary

  • Reducing Sugar: Sugar that can act as a reducing agent because it has a free anomeric carbon or can open to a reactive carbonyl form.
  • Non-Reducing Sugar: Sugar without a free anomeric carbon, such as sucrose.
  • Oxidation: Loss of electrons, loss of hydrogen, or gain of oxygen.
  • Reduction: Gain of electrons or hydrogen.
  • Hydrolysis: Bond-breaking reaction using water.
  • Condensation: Bond-forming reaction that releases water.
  • Isomer: Molecule with the same formula but different structure.
  • Mutarotation: Interconversion between alpha and beta sugar forms in solution.
  • Glycosidic Bond: Bond that connects sugar units.
