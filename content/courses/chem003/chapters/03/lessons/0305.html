Chapter 3 Summary: Carbohydrates

In this chapter, you learned about carbohydrates: their nature, classification, structure, cyclization, glycosidic bonds, disaccharides, polysaccharides, and key chemical properties.

1. Nature and Classification of Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are biomolecules made mainly of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen. Many simple carbohydrates follow the approximate formula Cn(H2O)n.

Big idea: Carbohydrates are commonly described as polyhydroxy aldehydes or polyhydroxy ketones.
Classification Description Examples
Monosaccharide One sugar unit Glucose, fructose, ribose
Disaccharide Two sugar units Sucrose, lactose, maltose
Oligosaccharide Short chain of sugar units Cell-surface carbohydrate chains
Polysaccharide Long chain of many sugar units Starch, glycogen, cellulose, chitin

2. Monosaccharides and Cyclization

Monosaccharides are the simplest carbohydrates. In water, many monosaccharides do not remain mostly in open-chain form. Instead, they form cyclic structures.

Concept Meaning
Open-chain form Straight-chain sugar form containing a carbonyl group.
Cyclic form Ring form produced when an internal hydroxyl group reacts with the carbonyl group.
Anomeric carbon The former carbonyl carbon that becomes a new stereocenter after ring formation.
Alpha and beta forms Anomers that differ in the position of the OH group on the anomeric carbon.
Mutarotation Interconversion between alpha and beta forms through the open-chain form.
Important: The difference between α-glucose and β-glucose affects the structure and function of larger carbohydrates such as starch and cellulose.

3. Disaccharides and Polysaccharides

Monosaccharides join through glycosidic bonds. These bonds form through condensation reactions and are broken by hydrolysis.

Disaccharide Components Common Source
Maltose Glucose + glucose Starch breakdown, germinating grains
Sucrose Glucose + fructose Table sugar, sugar cane, sugar beets
Lactose Glucose + galactose Milk and dairy products
Polysaccharide Main Function Where Found
Starch Plant energy storage Plants
Glycogen Animal energy storage Liver and muscles
Cellulose Structural support Plant cell walls
Chitin Structural support Fungi and arthropods
Key comparison: Starch and cellulose are both made of glucose, but starch has α-linkages while cellulose has β-linkages.

4. Chemical Properties of Carbohydrates

Carbohydrate reactions are controlled by hydroxyl groups, carbonyl groups, glycosidic bonds, and the anomeric carbon.

Property or Reaction Explanation Example
Reducing sugar behavior A sugar can act as a reducing agent if it has a free anomeric carbon or can open to a reactive carbonyl form. Glucose, maltose, lactose
Non-reducing sugar No free anomeric carbon is available. Sucrose
Oxidation Carbonyl group can be oxidized. Glucose → gluconic acid
Reduction Carbonyl group can be reduced to alcohol. Glucose → sorbitol
Hydrolysis Water breaks glycosidic bonds. Sucrose → glucose + fructose
Condensation Sugars join and release water. Monosaccharides → disaccharide
Isomerization Sugars with the same formula may have different structures. Glucose and fructose

5. High-Yield Comparisons

Pair Difference
Glucose vs Fructose Glucose is an aldose; fructose is a ketose.
Maltose vs Sucrose Maltose is reducing; sucrose is non-reducing.
Starch vs Glycogen Both store glucose, but glycogen is more highly branched.
Starch vs Cellulose Both are glucose polymers, but starch has α-linkages while cellulose has β-linkages.
Condensation vs Hydrolysis Condensation builds bonds and releases water; hydrolysis breaks bonds using water.

6. Quick Review Checkpoints

Checkpoint 1

What bond connects monosaccharides together?

Glycosidic bond.

Checkpoint 2

Why is sucrose considered non-reducing?

Because both anomeric carbons are involved in the glycosidic bond.

Checkpoint 3

Why can humans digest starch but not cellulose?

Humans can digest α-glycosidic bonds in starch, but not β-glycosidic bonds in cellulose.

Checkpoint 4

Which reaction breaks glycosidic bonds during digestion?

Hydrolysis.

Checkpoint 5

What is mutarotation?

The interconversion between alpha and beta sugar forms through the open-chain form.

7. Key Terms Summary

  • Carbohydrate: Biomolecule made mainly of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen.
  • Monosaccharide: Single sugar unit.
  • Disaccharide: Two sugar units joined by a glycosidic bond.
  • Polysaccharide: Long chain of many sugar units.
  • Glycosidic Bond: Bond linking sugar units.
  • Condensation: Reaction that forms a bond and releases water.
  • Hydrolysis: Reaction that breaks a bond using water.
  • Anomeric Carbon: Carbon involved in alpha/beta forms and reducing sugar behavior.
  • Reducing Sugar: Sugar with a free anomeric carbon or reactive carbonyl form.
  • Non-Reducing Sugar: Sugar with no free anomeric carbon.
  • Mutarotation: Interconversion of alpha and beta forms.
  • Isomer: Molecule with the same formula but different structure.
