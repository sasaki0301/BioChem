Lesson 1: Nature and Classification of Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are one of the major biomolecules of life. They serve as energy sources, structural materials, and recognition molecules in living organisms.

Overview — What You Will Learn

  • What carbohydrates are and why they are important
  • The general composition of carbohydrates
  • How carbohydrates are classified by size and functional group
  • The difference between monosaccharides, disaccharides, oligosaccharides, and polysaccharides
  • How carbohydrates function in energy, structure, and cell recognition
Big Idea: Carbohydrates are not “just sugar.” They include simple sugars like glucose, storage molecules like starch and glycogen, and structural materials like cellulose.

1. What Are Carbohydrates?

Carbohydrates are organic compounds made mainly of carbon (C), hydrogen (H), and oxygen (O). Many simple carbohydrates follow the approximate formula:

General formula: Cn(H2O)n

This formula is why they are called “carbohydrates,” which roughly means hydrates of carbon. For example:

  • Glucose: C6H12O6
  • Fructose: C6H12O6
  • Ribose: C5H10O5

However, not all carbohydrates perfectly follow this formula, especially modified carbohydrates found in cells.

Quick Check: Why are carbohydrates called “hydrates of carbon”?
Because many simple carbohydrates have a ratio that looks like carbon plus water units, approximately Cn(H2O)n.

2. Why Are Carbohydrates Important?

Carbohydrates have several major biological roles. The most familiar role is providing energy, but they also support cell structure and communication.

Function Explanation Example
Energy Source Cells break down carbohydrates to produce ATP. Glucose used in cellular respiration
Energy Storage Organisms store glucose in larger carbohydrate molecules. Starch in plants, glycogen in animals
Structural Support Some carbohydrates form strong fibers and protective structures. Cellulose in plant cell walls, chitin in fungi/insects
Cell Recognition Carbohydrates on cell surfaces help cells identify and communicate with each other. Glycoproteins and glycolipids in membranes
Quick Check: Which carbohydrate stores energy in animals?
Glycogen stores glucose in animals, especially in the liver and muscles.

3. Basic Structure of Carbohydrates

Most carbohydrates contain:

  • Many hydroxyl groups (−OH)
  • One carbonyl group, either as an aldehyde or ketone

These functional groups make carbohydrates highly polar and able to form hydrogen bonds with water.

Most simple carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or polyhydroxy ketones.
Important definition: A carbohydrate is commonly described as a polyhydroxy aldehyde or polyhydroxy ketone, or a compound that can yield these when hydrolyzed.
Quick Check: What two major functional features are commonly found in simple carbohydrates?
Many hydroxyl groups (−OH) and one carbonyl group (C=O).

4. Classification by Number of Sugar Units

Carbohydrates are commonly classified based on how many sugar units they contain.

Class Number of Sugar Units Examples Simple Explanation
Monosaccharide 1 Glucose, fructose, galactose, ribose Single sugar unit; cannot be hydrolyzed into smaller carbohydrates.
Disaccharide 2 Sucrose, lactose, maltose Two monosaccharides joined together.
Oligosaccharide 3–10 Cell-surface carbohydrate chains Short chains often involved in recognition and signaling.
Polysaccharide Many Starch, glycogen, cellulose, chitin Long chains used for storage or structure.
Quick Check: Is sucrose a monosaccharide or disaccharide?
Sucrose is a disaccharide because it contains two sugar units: glucose and fructose.

5. Monosaccharides: The Simplest Carbohydrates

Monosaccharides are the simplest carbohydrates. They are the building blocks of larger carbohydrates.

Common Monosaccharides

  • Glucose: main energy sugar in blood
  • Fructose: sugar found in fruits and honey
  • Galactose: part of lactose in milk
  • Ribose: part of RNA
  • Deoxyribose: part of DNA
Key idea: Monosaccharides are like “single building blocks.” Disaccharides and polysaccharides are made by linking these blocks together.
Quick Check: What monosaccharide is commonly known as blood sugar?
Glucose is commonly called blood sugar.

6. Classification by Carbonyl Group: Aldoses and Ketoses

Monosaccharides can also be classified based on the type of carbonyl group they contain.

Type Carbonyl Position Example Explanation
Aldose Carbonyl at the end of the chain Glucose, galactose, ribose Contains an aldehyde group in the open-chain form.
Ketose Carbonyl inside the chain Fructose Contains a ketone group in the open-chain form.
The position of the carbonyl group helps classify monosaccharides as aldoses or ketoses.
Quick Check: Is fructose an aldose or ketose?
Fructose is a ketose because its carbonyl group is inside the carbon chain.

7. Classification by Number of Carbon Atoms

Monosaccharides can also be named based on the number of carbon atoms they contain.

Number of Carbons Name Example
3 Triose Glyceraldehyde
4 Tetrose Erythrose
5 Pentose Ribose, deoxyribose
6 Hexose Glucose, fructose, galactose
Example: Glucose is an aldohexose. This means it has an aldehyde group and six carbon atoms.
Quick Check: Ribose has five carbon atoms. What type of monosaccharide is it by carbon number?
Ribose is a pentose.

8. Disaccharides and Polysaccharides: Built from Monosaccharides

When monosaccharides join, they form larger carbohydrates.

Disaccharides

  • Maltose: glucose + glucose
  • Sucrose: glucose + fructose
  • Lactose: glucose + galactose

Polysaccharides

  • Starch: plant energy storage
  • Glycogen: animal energy storage
  • Cellulose: plant structural support
  • Chitin: structural support in fungi and arthropods
Simple sugars join by condensation reactions to form larger carbohydrates.
Quick Check: What is the storage polysaccharide in animals?
Glycogen is the main storage polysaccharide in animals.

9. Reducing and Non-Reducing Sugars

Some carbohydrates are classified as reducing sugars because they can act as mild reducing agents. This usually depends on whether the sugar has a free carbonyl group or can open into a form that has one.

  • Reducing sugars: glucose, lactose, maltose
  • Non-reducing sugar: sucrose
Simple way to remember: A sugar is reducing if it has a free anomeric carbon that can open into a reactive carbonyl form.
Quick Check: Is sucrose usually considered a reducing sugar?
No. Sucrose is non-reducing because both anomeric carbons are involved in the glycosidic bond.

Why are carbohydrates highly soluble in water, especially small sugars?

Activity 5: Application

A molecule is described as a six-carbon sugar with an aldehyde group. What classification fits it best?

It is an aldohexose. “Aldo-” means aldehyde group, and “hexose” means six carbons.

12. Key Terms Summary

  • Carbohydrate: Organic biomolecule made mainly of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen.
  • Monosaccharide: Single sugar unit.
  • Disaccharide: Two monosaccharides joined together.
  • Oligosaccharide: Short chain of 3–10 sugar units.
  • Polysaccharide: Long chain of many sugar units.
  • Aldose: Monosaccharide with an aldehyde group.
  • Ketose: Monosaccharide with a ketone group.
  • Pentose: Five-carbon sugar.
  • Hexose: Six-carbon sugar.
  • Reducing Sugar: Sugar that can act as a reducing agent due to a free reactive carbonyl/anomeric carbon.
