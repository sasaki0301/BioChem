Lesson 2: Fatty Acids and Triglycerides

Fatty acids and triglycerides are important lipid molecules involved in energy storage, insulation, membrane structure, and metabolism.

Overview — What You Will Learn

  • What fatty acids are and how they are structured
  • The difference between saturated and unsaturated fatty acids
  • How chain length and double bonds affect melting point
  • What triglycerides are and how they are formed
  • The difference between fats and oils
  • Why triglycerides are efficient long-term energy storage molecules
Big Idea: Fatty acids are lipid building blocks, while triglycerides are storage lipids made from glycerol and three fatty acids.

1. What Are Fatty Acids?

A fatty acid is a long hydrocarbon chain with a carboxylic acid group at one end. The hydrocarbon chain is mostly nonpolar, while the carboxyl group is polar and acidic.

This structure gives fatty acids two important parts:

  • Hydrocarbon tail: long nonpolar chain, hydrophobic
  • Carboxyl head: −COOH group, polar and acidic
Simple structure: CH3−(CH2)n−COOH

Lipid molecules often have hydrophobic hydrocarbon regions that interact poorly with water.

Quick Check: Which part of a fatty acid is hydrophobic?
The long hydrocarbon tail is hydrophobic because it is mostly nonpolar.

2. Saturated and Unsaturated Fatty Acids

Fatty acids are commonly classified based on whether their carbon chain contains carbon-carbon double bonds.

Type Structure Shape Typical Physical State
Saturated Fatty Acid No carbon-carbon double bonds Straight chain Often solid at room temperature
Unsaturated Fatty Acid One or more carbon-carbon double bonds Usually bent if cis double bond is present Often liquid at room temperature

Saturated Fatty Acids

Saturated fatty acids are “saturated” with hydrogen atoms. Because they have no double bonds, their chains are usually straight. Straight chains can pack tightly together, increasing melting point.

Unsaturated Fatty Acids

Unsaturated fatty acids contain at least one double bond. Most natural unsaturated fatty acids have cis double bonds, which create bends in the chain. These bends prevent tight packing, lowering the melting point.

Memory Tip: Saturated = straight = tightly packed = often solid. Unsaturated = bent = loosely packed = often liquid.
Quick Check: Why are many plant oils liquid at room temperature?
Many plant oils contain unsaturated fatty acids with cis double bonds, which prevent tight packing and lower melting point.

3. Mono- and Polyunsaturated Fatty Acids

Unsaturated fatty acids can be classified further based on the number of double bonds.

Type Number of Double Bonds Example
Monounsaturated Fatty Acid One double bond Oleic acid
Polyunsaturated Fatty Acid Two or more double bonds Linoleic acid, linolenic acid, arachidonic acid

Polyunsaturated fatty acids are important in cell membranes and signaling. Some are called essential fatty acids because the body cannot make them in sufficient amounts and they must be obtained from the diet.

Quick Check: What does polyunsaturated mean?
It means the fatty acid has two or more carbon-carbon double bonds.

4. Cis and Trans Fatty Acids

Double bonds can create different shapes depending on the arrangement of atoms around the double bond.

Type Shape Effect on Packing
Cis Fatty Acid Bent chain Packs poorly; lower melting point
Trans Fatty Acid More linear chain Packs more like saturated fats; higher melting point

Most naturally occurring unsaturated fatty acids are cis. Trans fats can be produced during partial hydrogenation and are associated with negative health effects.

Quick Check: Which type of double bond creates a bend in fatty acid chains?
Cis double bonds create bends in fatty acid chains.

5. Chain Length and Melting Point

Fatty acid melting point is affected by both chain length and degree of saturation.

Factor Effect on Melting Point Reason
Longer chain Higher melting point Stronger van der Waals interactions
More saturation Higher melting point Straighter chains pack tightly
More cis double bonds Lower melting point Bends prevent tight packing
Example: Butter is more solid because it contains more saturated fatty acids. Many vegetable oils are liquid because they contain more unsaturated fatty acids.
Quick Check: Which would likely have a higher melting point: a saturated fatty acid or an unsaturated fatty acid of the same length?
The saturated fatty acid would likely have a higher melting point because it packs more tightly.

6. What Are Triglycerides?

A triglyceride, also called a triacylglycerol, is a lipid made from one glycerol molecule attached to three fatty acids.

Glycerol has three hydroxyl groups, and each hydroxyl group can react with the carboxyl group of a fatty acid to form an ester bond.

Basic structure: Glycerol + 3 fatty acids → Triglyceride + 3 water molecules
Component Role
Glycerol Three-carbon alcohol backbone
Fatty acids Long hydrophobic chains attached to glycerol
Ester bonds Bonds connecting fatty acids to glycerol
Quick Check: How many fatty acids are attached to glycerol in a triglyceride?
Three fatty acids are attached to one glycerol molecule.

7. Formation of Triglycerides

Triglycerides form through a condensation reaction. Each fatty acid forms an ester bond with glycerol, and water is released.

Step What Happens?
1 The −OH group of glycerol reacts with the −COOH group of a fatty acid.
2 An ester bond forms.
3 Water is released.
4 This happens three times to form a triglyceride.
Important: Triglyceride formation is similar in logic to ester formation from carboxylic acids and alcohols.
Quick Check: What type of bond connects fatty acids to glycerol?
Ester bonds connect fatty acids to glycerol.

8. Hydrolysis of Triglycerides

Triglycerides can be broken down by hydrolysis. Water is used to break the ester bonds, releasing glycerol and fatty acids.

Hydrolysis reaction:
Triglyceride + 3 H2O → Glycerol + 3 fatty acids

In digestion, enzymes called lipases help hydrolyze triglycerides into smaller absorbable components.

Quick Check: What enzyme helps digest triglycerides?
Lipase helps digest triglycerides by hydrolyzing ester bonds.

9. Why Are Triglycerides Good Energy Storage Molecules?

Triglycerides are excellent long-term energy storage molecules because they are highly reduced and hydrophobic.

Property Why It Helps Energy Storage
Many C−H bonds Release large amounts of energy when oxidized.
Hydrophobic Stored without carrying much water, making storage compact.
Large nonpolar chains Contain many energy-rich bonds.
Energy comparison: Lipids store more energy per gram than carbohydrates because they contain more reduced carbon-hydrogen bonds and less oxygen.
Quick Check: Why is fat storage more compact than glycogen storage?
Triglycerides are hydrophobic and stored with little water, while glycogen is hydrated and stored with water.

10. Fats vs Oils

The terms “fat” and “oil” often refer to triglycerides, but they differ in physical state at room temperature.

Feature Fat Oil
State at room temperature Usually solid Usually liquid
Fatty acid content Often more saturated fatty acids Often more unsaturated fatty acids
Chain packing Chains pack tightly Chains pack loosely due to bends
Example Butter, animal fat Olive oil, corn oil, fish oil
Quick Check: Why is butter usually solid while olive oil is liquid?
Butter contains more saturated fatty acids that pack tightly, while olive oil contains more unsaturated fatty acids that pack less tightly.

11. Biological Importance

Fatty acids and triglycerides are important in both structure and metabolism.

Molecule Main Biological Importance
Fatty acids Building blocks of triglycerides, phospholipids, and signaling molecules.
Essential fatty acids Must be obtained from diet; used in membranes and signaling.
Triglycerides Main long-term energy storage form in animals.
Stored fat Provides insulation and cushioning for organs.

14. Key Terms Summary

  • Fatty Acid: Long hydrocarbon chain with a carboxylic acid group.
  • Saturated Fatty Acid: Fatty acid with no carbon-carbon double bonds.
  • Unsaturated Fatty Acid: Fatty acid with one or more carbon-carbon double bonds.
  • Monounsaturated: Has one double bond.
  • Polyunsaturated: Has two or more double bonds.
  • Cis Fatty Acid: Unsaturated fatty acid with a bent chain.
  • Trans Fatty Acid: Unsaturated fatty acid with a more linear chain.
  • Triglyceride: Lipid made of glycerol and three fatty acids.
  • Ester Bond: Bond connecting fatty acids to glycerol.
  • Lipase: Enzyme that helps hydrolyze triglycerides.
  • Essential Fatty Acid: Fatty acid that must be obtained from the diet.
