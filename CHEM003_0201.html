  • A carbonyl in the middle → ketone

2. Physical Properties & Biological Relevance

How polarity affects each group:

  • Alcohols: strong hydrogen bonding → higher boiling points
  • Ethers: polar but cannot H-bond donate → lower b.p.
  • Thiols: weak H-bonding → lower b.p., strong odors
  • Aldehydes & Ketones: polar C=O → reactive & water-soluble (small carbonyls)

Examples:

  • Ethanol: miscible with water due to −OH
  • Acetone: a common solvent; small and fully miscible
  • Cysteine (thiol): forms disulfide bridges in proteins

3. Important Reactions — Detailed Steps

A. Oxidation of Alcohols

Primary alcohols oxidize first to aldehydes and then to carboxylic acids.

Step 1 — Primary alcohol → Aldehyde (PCC)

Primary alcohol oxidized to aldehyde using PCC (mild oxidant).

Step 2 — Aldehyde → Carboxylic acid (KMnO₄)

Aldehydes can continue oxidation to carboxylic acids under strong conditions.

Secondary alcohols oxidize to ketones; tertiary alcohols resist oxidation.

B. Williamson Ether Synthesis (making ethers)

Why it works: Convert an alcohol to an alkoxide (strong nucleophile) then perform an SN2 substitution on a primary alkyl halide to form an ether.

Step 1 — Formation of alkoxide

A strong base (e.g., NaH) deprotonates the alcohol to give an alkoxide nucleophile and hydrogen gas.

Step 2 — SN2 attack on alkyl halide (Williamson)

SN2 substitution yields an ether. Use primary R'–X for best yields and to avoid elimination.

C. Oxidation of Thiols → Disulfide (protein chemistry example)

Why it matters: Two −SH groups on cysteine residues can be oxidized to a disulfide bond (—S—S—), stabilizing protein structure.

Oxidation of thiols forms disulfide bonds (cystine) important for tertiary/quaternary structure.

D. Nucleophilic Addition to Carbonyls (basic idea)

Why carbonyls react: The carbonyl carbon is electrophilic—nucleophiles attack, producing tetrahedral intermediates that are protonated.

Nucleophiles add to carbonyls giving oxyanion intermediates; protonation yields alcohol-type addition products.

4. Qualitative Indicators

These conceptual tests are useful for recognition (not to be performed outside a proper lab):

  • Tollens' concept: Aldehydes can reduce Ag⁺ to Ag(0) (silver mirror).
  • Fehling's concept: Aldehydes reduce Cu²⁺ to Cu₂O (red precipitate).
  • Lucas concept: Reactivity with HCl/ZnCl₂ differentiates primary/secondary/tertiary alcohols.
Reminder: These are conceptual descriptions only. Do not attempt chemical tests without proper lab supervision and safety.

Problem 3 — Mechanistic reasoning

Why are tertiary alcohols resistant to mild oxidation?

Tertiary alcohols lack an α-hydrogen on the carbon bearing −OH, so oxidation would require C–C bond cleavage (harsh conditions) rather than simple H removal.

Problem 4 — Boiling points

Which has higher boiling point: ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH) or dimethyl ether (CH₃OCH₃)? Explain.

Ethanol — it can form intermolecular hydrogen bonds; dimethyl ether cannot donate H-bonds.

6. Key Terms Summary

  • Alcohol (−OH): polar, H-bonding; named with −ol.
  • Ether (R−O−R'): oxygen bridge; named as alkoxyalkane or common names.
  • Thiol (−SH): sulfur analog of alcohol; forms disulfides.
  • Aldehyde (−CHO): terminal carbonyl; oxidizable to acids.
  • Ketone (RC(=O)R'): internal carbonyl; product of secondary alcohol oxidation.
  • Williamson synthesis: alkoxide SN2 on an alkyl halide to form ethers.
