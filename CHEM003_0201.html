Lesson 1: Alcohols, Ethers, Thiols, Ketones & Aldehydes

This lesson covers functional groups derived from hydrocarbons: alcohols, ethers, thiols, aldehydes, and ketones. We’ll study their structure, naming, physical properties, common reactions, biological relevance, and worked practice problems.

1. Structures & Nomenclature

GroupGeneral StructureNaming & Example
Alcohol (hydroxyl) R−OH IUPAC: replace −e of alkane with −ol (e.g., ethanol).
Common: methyl alcohol = methanol.
Example: CH₃CH₂OH — ethanol.
Ether R−O−R′ IUPAC: name as alkoxyalkane (e.g., methoxyethane) or common: ethyl methyl ether.
Example: CH₃−O−CH₂CH₃ — ethyl methyl ether.
Thiol (sulfhydryl) R−SH IUPAC: suffix −thiol (e.g., ethanethiol).
Example: CH₃CH₂SH — ethanethiol.
Aldehyde R−CHO IUPAC: replace −e with −al (e.g., ethanal). Aldehydes have carbonyl at end of chain.
Example: CH₃CHO — ethanal (acetaldehyde).
Ketone R−C(=O)−R′ IUPAC: replace −e with −one and number carbonyl position if needed (e.g., propanone = acetone).
Example: CH₃COCH₃ — propanone (acetone).
Quick recognition tips:
  • Aldehydes have the carbonyl (C=O) at the end and often react with mild oxidants.
  • Ketones have carbonyl within the chain — generally less easily oxidized than aldehydes.
  • Alcohols are polar and form hydrogen bonds; ethers are less polar and have lower boiling points than equivalent alcohols.
  • Thiol compounds are less polar, often have strong odors, and can form disulfide bonds (important in proteins).

2. Physical Properties & Biological Relevance

Polarity & hydrogen bonding:

  • Alcohols (−OH): polar, form hydrogen bonds → higher boiling points and water solubility (short chains).
  • Ethers (R−O−R): do not form hydrogen bonds between themselves (no −OH proton) → lower b.p. than alcohols but can accept H-bonds from water.
  • Thiols (−SH): less polar than alcohols; weaker hydrogen bonding; distinctive odors (e.g., skunk smell molecules are thiols).
  • Aldehydes & ketones (C=O): polar; carbonyl oxygen accepts H-bonds from water, increasing solubility for small molecules.

Biological examples

  • Alcohol: Ethanol (fermentation); serine contains −OH in side chain.
  • Ether: Ether linkages appear in some lipids and in lipid-linked sugars.
  • Thiol: Cysteine side chain −SH forms disulfide bonds (—S—S—) in proteins, stabilizing tertiary/quaternary structure.
  • Aldehyde & Ketone: Sugars contain carbonyl groups: glucose (aldehyde in open chain), fructose (ketone).

3. Important Reactions — Step by step

A. Oxidation of Alcohols

Primary alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid
Secondary alcohol → ketone
Tertiary alcohol → resists oxidation (under normal conditions)

  1. Step 1 (Primary → Aldehyde): remove 2 H (one from −OH, one from α-C) using mild oxidant (e.g., PCC) → aldehyde.
  2. Step 2 (Aldehyde → Carboxylic acid): further oxidation (strong oxidants like KMnO₄) adds oxygen to form −COOH.
Example (stepwise): 
CH₃CH₂OH  --[PCC]-->  CH₃CHO  --[KMnO₄]-->  CH₃COOH
Note: Secondary alcohols (R₂CHOH) oxidize to ketones (R₂C=O). Tertiary alcohols lack an α-H and generally do not oxidize under mild conditions.

B. Ether formation (Williamson ether synthesis)

Step-by-step mechanism (simplified):

  1. Deprotonate an alcohol with a strong base (e.g., NaH or NaOH) → alkoxide (R−O⁻).
  2. Alkoxide performs nucleophilic substitution (SN2) on a primary alkyl halide (R′−X) → R−O−R′ (ether) + X⁻.
Example: 
CH₃CH₂O⁻  +  CH₃Br  --->  CH₃CH₂OCH₃  +  Br⁻
Tip: SN2 works best with primary alkyl halides; secondary/tertiary lead to elimination or poor yields.

C. Oxidation of Thiols & Disulfide formation

Two cysteine −SH groups can oxidize to form a disulfide bond (—S—S—), important in protein folding.

2 R−SH  --[oxidant]-->  R−S−S−R  +  2 H⁺ + 2 e⁻

D. Reactions of Carbonyls — Nucleophilic Addition

Carbonyl carbon (C=O) is electrophilic. Nucleophiles (Nu⁻) can add to it:

  1. Nucleophile attacks C=O → tetrahedral intermediate.
  2. Depending on reagents, the intermediate can be protonated to give alcohol derivatives (e.g., in addition of H⁻ or R−Li), or undergo condensation reactions.

4. Simple laboratory tests (qualitative)

Useful to recognize functional groups in the lab (conceptual overview):

  • Tollen's test: aldehydes reduce Tollens' reagent (Ag⁺) to metallic silver (silver mirror) — ketones generally do not.
  • Fehling's test: aldehydes reduce Cu²⁺ (blue) to Cu₂O (red precipitate) — useful for reducing sugars (open-chain aldehydes).
  • Lucas test: differentiates primary, secondary, tertiary alcohols based on reactivity with HCl/ZnCl₂ (tertiary reacts fastest).
Safety note: These tests involve reagents that must be handled with care in a proper lab setting — conceptual understanding is sufficient for foundational biochemistry.

5. Practice Problems (try first — answers hidden)

Problem 1 — Naming

Give the IUPAC name for each compound:

  1. CH₃CH₂CH₂OH
  2. CH₃−CO−CH₂CH₃
  3. CH₃CH₂−S−H
  1. 1-Propanol (or propan-1-ol)
  2. Butan-2-one (commonly: methyl ethyl ketone) — actually CH₃COCH₂CH₃ = butan-2-one
  3. Ethanethiol (or ethane-thiol)

Problem 2 — Reaction prediction

Predict major product:

  1. Oxidize primary alcohol CH₃CH₂CH₂OH with PCC.
  2. React sodium ethoxide (CH₃CH₂O⁻ Na⁺) with methyl bromide (CH₃Br).
  3. Two cysteine residues in a protein exposed to an oxidizing environment — what bond forms?
  1. PCC (mild oxidant) converts primary alcohol → aldehyde: CH₃CH₂CHO (propanal).
  2. SN2 substitution gives an ether: CH₃CH₂OCH₃ (ethyl methyl ether).
  3. Disulfide bond (—S—S—) between the two cysteine side chains (cystine formation).

Problem 3 — Mechanism sketch (step-by-step)

Sketch the key steps (word description is fine) for the Williamson ether synthesis of ethyl methyl ether starting from ethanol.

  1. Deprotonation: Ethanol + NaH → ethoxide ion (CH₃CH₂O⁻) + H₂.
  2. Nucleophilic attack: CH₃CH₂O⁻ attacks CH₃Br (methyl bromide) in an SN2 step → CH₃CH₂OCH₃ + Br⁻.
  3. Overall: formation of ethyl methyl ether via SN2 substitution.

Problem 4 — Compare properties

Which has higher boiling point: ethanol or dimethyl ether (CH₃OCH₃)? Explain briefly.

Ethanol has higher boiling point because it can form intermolecular hydrogen bonds (−OH groups), while dimethyl ether cannot donate H-bonds (only accept), leading to weaker intermolecular attraction.

7. Key Terms Summary

  • Alcohol (−OH): hydroxyl functional group; polar, H-bonding.
  • Ether (R−O−R′): oxygen bridge between two alkyl/aryl groups; lower b.p. than alcohols.
  • Thiol (−SH): sulfur analog of alcohol; can form disulfide bonds.
  • Aldehyde (−CHO): carbonyl at chain end; easily oxidized.
  • Ketone (R−CO−R′): carbonyl within chain; polar but less easily oxidized.
  • Williamson synthesis: method to form ethers using alkoxides and alkyl halides (SN2).
  • Oxidation: increase in oxidation state; in organic chemistry often loss of H or gain of O.
