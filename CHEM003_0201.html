Lesson 1: Alcohols, Ethers, Thiols, Ketones & Aldehydes

This lesson covers alcohols, ethers, thiols, aldehydes, and ketones. It includes clear step-by-step reaction explanations with small illustrated schemes after each step so beginners can follow the transformations and logic.

1. Structures & Nomenclature

GroupGeneral StructureNaming & Example
Alcohol (hydroxyl)R−OHIUPAC: replace −e with −ol (ethanol). Example: CH₃CH₂OH — ethanol.
EtherR−O−R′IUPAC: alkoxyalkane (methoxyethane); common: ethyl methyl ether. Example: CH₃OCH₂CH₃.
Thiol (sulfhydryl)R−SHIUPAC: −thiol (ethanethiol). Example: CH₃CH₂SH.
AldehydeR−CHOIUPAC: −al (ethanal). Carbonyl is terminal. Example: CH₃CHO.
KetoneR−C(=O)−R′IUPAC: −one (propanone). Carbonyl is internal. Example: CH₃COCH₃.
Recognition tips for beginners
  • If you see −OH attached to carbon → alcohol.
  • If you see C=O at the end of a chain → aldehyde; if internal → ketone.
  • Thiol is sulfur analog of alcohol (−SH) and often smells strong.

2. Physical Properties & Biological Relevance

How polarity and hydrogen bonding affect behavior:

  • Alcohols: Polar, hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors → higher b.p. and good solubility for short chains.
  • Ethers: Polar but cannot donate H-bonds; lower b.p. than comparable alcohols.
  • Thiol: Less polar; weaker H-bonding; characteristic odors; can form disulfide bonds in proteins (important structurally).
  • Aldehydes & Ketones: Carbonyl oxygen is polar and can accept H-bonds → small ones are water-soluble; carbonyl carbon is electrophilic (reactive).

Examples:

  • Ethanol: used in fermentation and solvents; small alcohols are miscible with water.
  • Acetone (propanone): small ketone used as solvent; miscible with water.
  • Cysteine: contains −SH and forms disulfide bridges that stabilize proteins.

Hydrogen-bonding capability strongly influences boiling point and solubility.

3. Important Reactions — Detailed steps with illustrations

A. Oxidation of Alcohols (Primary → Aldehyde → Carboxylic acid)

Why it happens: Oxidation removes hydrogen (or adds oxygen). Primary alcohols have two H atoms on the carbon bearing −OH (an α-H), so they can be oxidized first to aldehydes and then further to acids.

Step 1 — Primary alcohol → Aldehyde (mild oxidant, e.g., PCC)

CH₃CH₂CH₂OH --[PCC]--> CH₃CH₂CHO + 2 H⁺ + 2 e⁻

Illustration explanation: The reagent removes two hydrogens: one from the −OH (as a proton H⁺) and one from the carbon (as H⁻ equivalent). The C–O bond becomes C=O (carbonyl) forming an aldehyde. PCC is a mild oxidant that stops at the aldehyde stage (it does not further oxidize under these conditions).

Step 2 — Aldehyde → Carboxylic acid (strong oxidant, e.g., KMnO₄)

CH₃CH₂CHO --[KMnO₄ / strong oxidant]--> CH₃CH₂COOH

Illustration explanation: The aldehyde (which has a hydrogen on the carbonyl carbon) is further oxidized: the carbonyl carbon gains an additional oxygen (or loses electrons) to form the carboxyl group −COOH. Strong oxidants supply the required oxygen or remove electrons to allow this transformation.

Note for beginners: Secondary alcohols (R₂CHOH) convert to ketones (R₂C=O) because they lack the hydrogen on the carbonyl carbon required for further oxidation. Tertiary alcohols do not oxidize easily because there is no α-H to remove under normal conditions.

B. Williamson Ether Synthesis (making ethers)

Why it works: You convert an alcohol to a stronger nucleophile (an alkoxide) then perform an SN2 substitution on a suitable alkyl halide to form an ether.

Step 1 — Formation of alkoxide

ROH + NaH --> R O⁻ Na⁺ + H₂(g)

Illustration explanation: A strong base (like sodium hydride) deprotonates the alcohol to form an alkoxide ion (R−O⁻). This alkoxide is a good nucleophile because it carries a negative charge on oxygen.

Step 2 — SN2 nucleophilic substitution on primary alkyl halide

R O⁻ + R'−X --SN2--> R−O−R' + X⁻

Illustration explanation: The alkoxide back-side attacks the electrophilic carbon of the alkyl halide (preferably primary for good SN2), displacing the halide ion X⁻ and forming the ether R−O−R'. SN2 gives inversion of configuration at that carbon if it is chiral — useful point for stereochemistry.

C. Oxidation of Thiols → Disulfide (protein chemistry example)

Why it matters: Two −SH groups on cysteine residues in proteins can be oxidized to form a covalent disulfide bond (—S—S—) that stabilizes tertiary and quaternary structures.

Reaction (conceptual):

2 R−SH --[oxidant]--> R−S−S−R + 2 H⁺ + 2 e⁻

Illustration explanation: The sulfur atoms lose electrons (are oxidized) and form a covalent bond connecting the two cysteine side chains. In cells, this process can be reversible (disulfide interchange) and is part of folding pathways and redox regulation.

D. Nucleophilic Addition to Carbonyls (basic idea)

Why carbonyls react: The carbonyl carbon is electron-poor (electrophilic) because oxygen pulls electron density away; nucleophiles can attack this carbon forming tetrahedral intermediates.

Step — Nucleophile attack & protonation:

R−C(=O)−R' + Nu⁻ --> R−C(−Nu)(−O⁻)−R' --[H⁺]--> R−C(−Nu)(−OH)−R'

Illustration explanation: The nucleophile (Nu⁻) attacks the carbonyl carbon, breaking the π bond and giving an oxyanion intermediate. Protonation of the oxyanion (by solvent or an acid workup) yields the alcohol product or other addition product depending on Nu. For example, addition of H⁻ (from LiAlH₄) gives an alcohol; addition of HCN yields cyanohydrins.

4. Qualitative Indicators (conceptual tests)

These are conceptual chemical tests often discussed in textbooks to help identify functional groups conceptually — not instructions to perform experiments.

  • Tollens' concept (silver mirror): Aldehydes are oxidizable and can reduce Ag⁺ to Ag(0) in Tollens' reagent — ketones generally do not give this reaction.
  • Fehling's concept: Aldehydes can reduce blue Cu²⁺ to red Cu₂O — useful to illustrate reducing sugars (open-chain aldehyde forms).
  • Lucas concept: Reactivity with HCl/ZnCl₂ differentiates primary, secondary, and tertiary alcohols (tertiary reacts fastest due to carbocation stability in substitution).
Reminder: These tests are included for conceptual recognition only. They involve reagents that must be handled by trained personnel in proper labs; do not attempt them outside a controlled laboratory environment.

5. Practice Problems (try first — answers hidden)

Problem 1 — Naming (IUPAC)

Give the IUPAC name for each compound:

  1. CH₃CH₂CH₂OH
  2. CH₃COCH₂CH₃
  3. CH₃CH₂−S−H
  1. Propan-1-ol (or 1-propanol)
  2. Butan-2-one (also called methyl ethyl ketone for CH₃COCH₂CH₃)
  3. Ethanethiol (ethane-thiol)

Problem 2 — Reaction prediction

Predict the major product for each:

  1. Oxidize primary alcohol CH₃CH₂CH₂OH with PCC.
  2. React sodium ethoxide (CH₃CH₂O⁻ Na⁺) with methyl bromide (CH₃Br).
  3. Two cysteine residues in an oxidizing environment — what linkage forms?
  1. Propanal (CH₃CH₂CHO) — aldehyde (PCC stops at aldehyde).
  2. Ethyl methyl ether (CH₃CH₂OCH₃) — product of Williamson SN2.
  3. Disulfide bond (—S—S—) connects the two cysteine side chains (cystine).

Problem 3 — Mechanistic reasoning (short)

Explain briefly (2–3 sentences) why tertiary alcohols are resistant to oxidation under mild conditions.

Tertiary alcohols lack an α-hydrogen on the carbon bearing the −OH group (no H on the carbon to be removed). Oxidation typically requires removal of that hydrogen; without it, oxidation requires C–C bond cleavage which needs much harsher conditions.

Problem 4 — Compare boiling points

Which has a higher boiling point: ethanol (CH₃CH₂OH) or dimethyl ether (CH₃OCH₃)? Explain briefly.

Ethanol — because ethanol can form intermolecular hydrogen bonds (donates and accepts), while dimethyl ether cannot donate hydrogen bonds (only accept), resulting in weaker intermolecular attractions.

6. Key Terms Summary

  • Alcohol (−OH): polar, H-bonding; named with −ol suffix.
  • Ether (R−O−R'): oxygen bridge between groups; named as alkoxyalkane or common names.
  • Thiol (−SH): sulfur analog of alcohol; forms disulfides.
  • Aldehyde (−CHO): terminal carbonyl; oxidizable to acids.
  • Ketone (RC(=O)R'): internal carbonyl; forms by oxidation of secondary alcohols.
  • Williamson synthesis: formation of ethers via alkoxide SN2 on alkyl halide.
